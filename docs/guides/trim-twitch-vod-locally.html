How to trim Twitch VOD highlights locally

Twitch VODs are long, loud, and full of dead air. If you own the VOD (or have the streamer’s permission), you can download it, scan it locally, review the candidate timestamps, and cut clips without sending the whole thing to a cloud clipper.

Rights first: only download and edit a VOD you own or have explicit permission to use. Clipper does not include a Twitch downloader and does not bypass platform restrictions.

Step 1 — Get your VOD onto disk

Use the tool you already trust for your own content, such as TwitchDownloader or yt-dlp. The output is usually an .mp4 or .mkv; Clipper supports both, plus .mov and .avi.

# Example only; check the tool's current docs and your rights.
yt-dlp -o "vod.%(ext)s" "https://www.twitch.tv/videos/<id>"

Keep the original file. Clipper reads it and writes new clips; it does not modify the source.

Step 2 — Dry-run the VOD

clipper --input "D:\VODs\my-stream.mp4" --dry-run --pre 8 --post 5

The dry run writes:

  • a timestamped report (clipper-report-*.json),
  • preview JPEGs in trimmed\previews,
  • a ranked list of candidate windows with audio/motion scores.

Read it before exporting. A four-hour VOD might produce 30 candidates; you do not need all of them.

Step 3 — Tune for your stream

Stream audio is different from local gameplay capture. Music, alerts, and voice can dominate the mix. Two adjustments help:

  • Raise the motion weight if the audio is compressed or constant: --visual-weight 0.6 --audio-weight 0.4.
  • Raise --min-clip to avoid tiny reaction blips: --min-clip 4 or 5.
clipper --input "D:\VODs\my-stream.mp4" --dry-run --sensitivity 0.6 --visual-weight 0.6 --audio-weight 0.4 --min-clip 4

Step 4 — Export a review pack

clipper --input "D:\VODs\my-stream.mp4" --output "D:\VOD-clips" --pre 6 --post 4 --max-clips 20

Default export is stream copy: fast and lossless, but it starts at the nearest keyframe. For a VOD with a long keyframe interval, that can be several seconds of extra run-up. Use --reencode if the start matters.

clipper --input "D:\VODs\my-stream.mp4" --output "D:\VOD-clips" --reencode --encoder libx264 --crf 20

Step 5 — Review, rename, post

Open the clips in a player. Rename the keepers with a consistent pattern:

game_20261123_clip01_00m12s-00m18s.mp4

Keep the report JSON with the delivery if you are sending clips to an editor. It documents why each window was chosen and where the original timestamp is.

Local vs cloud VOD clippers

Cloud clippers are convenient if you want captions, vertical reframing, and publishing in one place. They also require uploading the VOD, an account, and often a subscription. A local pass is better when you want the raw highlights, no upload, and no per-minute cost.

They are not mutually exclusive. Use a local pass to find the 20 candidate windows, then send only those clips to a cloud editor for captions or vertical layouts. That reduces upload size and cost.

Limitations

  • No built-in downloader; use a tool you trust and have rights to use.
  • Heuristic detection: loud menus and alert sounds can score.
  • No captions or vertical crop in Clipper.
  • Stream copy can start early; re-encode for exact edges.
  • Very long VODs take time to decode; the proxy keeps memory low, not wall-clock time.

Try one VOD

Run a dry run on your own VOD, compare the timestamps to what you remember, and report the misses. That feedback is how the detector gets better.

FAQ

Can Clipper download Twitch VODs?

No. It scans a local file. Use a separate downloader for content you own or are allowed to use.

Will it find chat reactions?

No. It uses audio and visual signals, not chat. A chat-first tool may be better for pure reaction moments.

Can I batch multiple VODs?

Yes. Put them in one folder and use --input; add --recursive for subfolders.

Does it upload anything?

No. The UI binds to localhost and the CLI is local-only.
